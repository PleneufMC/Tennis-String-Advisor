Hybrid Tennis Strings: What Goes in the Mains, What Goes in the Crosses, and How Firm the Setup Will Be

In a hybrid setup, no measurement tells you which string belongs in the mains: you pick the direction for your game, and flipping it moves our firmness index by 0 to 2 points. A hybrid's firmness lands between those of its two strings: it is an estimate, and we only put a number on it when both strings' stiffness is lab-measured.

📅 November 23, 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read • 🎾 Strings

🎯 The short version

  • Which way round: there is no measured rule; flipped, our index moves by 0 to 2 points.
  • Firmness lands between the two strings: on an RA 64 racquet strung at 53 lb (24 kg) both ways, Ice Code in the mains with NRG2 in the crosses gives 29, Standard tier, between 31 for Ice Code alone and 25 for NRG2 alone.
  • The 60/40 split given to the mains is a site convention, not a measurement.
  • No number for natural gut, unmeasured strings or pre-packed sets.
  • A tension gap around the same average leaves the index unchanged.

🧵 1. Mains and Crosses: What a Hybrid Setup Is

The mains are the vertical strings in the bed, the crosses the horizontal ones. A hybrid puts one string each way; you string it from two separate sets of string, or buy a pre-packed hybrid. The string families themselves are compared in our polyester vs multifilament guide.

🎾 2. What Each Direction Does: Measured vs Habit

Three things have been measured: the mains sliding over the crosses, the friction between strings and, in the one test we found, the bed's stiffness both ways round; the rest is habit.

The mains slide, the crosses hold them

According to the Tennis Warehouse University (TWU) lab, the mains slide across the crosses when the ball pushes them sideways harder than friction can hold them; if they snap back during contact, the ball picks up more spin. In its test, gut mains with polyester crosses gave the lowest static friction and the reverse one of the highest; shaped or textured strings generally have more friction, polyesters the least.

That the mains break first is a stringer's observation (Holabird Sports, 2013), not a measurement.

Shaped mains, round crosses (poly/poly): a habit

For two polyesters, Jonas Eriksson (Tennisnerd, November 6, 2026, a disclosed partner of the Toroline brand) recommends a shaped string in the mains and a round, slick one in the crosses, which would help the mains move. TWU points the same way for the cross; we found no measurement of the pairing itself.

Which direction weighs more on firmness?

We found only one published test that compares both directions of the same hybrid (Tennis Warehouse, Klip X-Plosive, gut and co-polyester, undated): 76 stiffness units with the polyester in the mains, 70 with the gut. It shows the direction (the stiffer string in the mains makes the firmer bed), not how much each direction weighs, and those device units do not convert into our index. We found no published measurement of the mains' and crosses' shares.

📊 4. How Firm Is a Hybrid? What the Index Can Say

A hybrid lands between its two strings, in the middle or slightly closer to the mains when they are the stiffer string. It is an estimate drawn from two measured stiffness values, never a measurement of the setup.

How the site estimates a hybrid's index

Our firmness index (RCS) combines frame stiffness (RA), string stiffness (in lb/in) and tension. For a hybrid it makes two estimates at the average tension, one weighting the stiffness values 60% mains and 40% crosses, the other a plain average, and keeps the higher. The 60/40 split is a site convention, with no published measurement we could find behind it; it only applies when the mains are the stiffer string.

Six hybrids, with numbers

We only put numbers on hybrids whose two strings both have a stiffness aligned on a measurement by the TWU lab (†): 61 of the 179 strings in our database as of November 23, 2026.

The estimate can change tier: Ice Code goes from 31, Firm, on its own, to 29, Standard, with NRG2. The configurator starts at 53 lb (24 kg) in the mains and 48.5 lb (22 kg) in the crosses, on sliders in kilos. To get the table's figures, pick a racquet with an RA of 64 and set the "Mains" and "Crosses" sliders to 24 kg; leave them alone and Ice Code / NRG2 shows 28, not 29.

Flipping mains and crosses

Across the 1,826 pairs of measured strings with different stiffness, stiffer string in the mains compared with the other way round, the index stays put 1,086 times, moves by one point 723 times and by two points 17 times, never more. The tier changes in 162 cases: Razor Code / X-One Biphase gives 30, Firm, and 29, Standard, the other way round, where the plain average wins.

What we don't put a number on, and why

Without stiffness aligned on a measurement for both strings, a hybrid gets no index here: the figure would claim a precision it does not have. That covers every gut hybrid (the stiffness our database uses for gut is not aligned on a lab measurement), Babolat RPM Blast or Luxilon ALU Power, and the pre-packed sets with a published composition, none of which has both strings measured. Their composition, according to the brand or a retailer, either way round:

  • Babolat Hybrid RPM Blast + VS Touch: RPM Blast 1.25 and Touch VS 1.30 (Babolat);
  • Wilson Duo Power: ALU Power 1.25 and NXT Power 1.30 (Tennis Warehouse, Game-Set-Match);
  • Wilson Duo Control: 4G Rough 1.25 and NXT Control 16 (Tennis Warehouse);
  • Wilson Champion's Choice: Wilson natural gut 16 and ALU Power Rough 16L (Tennis Warehouse).

Their product pages show a stiffness derived from their two strings and say so; the configurator uses that figure: it is not a measurement of the set.

↔️ 5. Should Mains and Crosses Be Strung at Different Tensions?

We found no primary source that sets a gap: our string tension guide takes stock. For hybrids, the specialist press describes the habit of stringing the softer string tighter; more generally, according to Jonas Eriksson, many players string the crosses a little looser, and 4 lb (about 2 kg) is about as big a gap as you'll see. None of these habits is measured.

The index reads the average tension: Ice Code / NRG2 gives 29 at 53/53 lb (24/24 kg), and 28 at both 53/48.5 lb (24/22 kg) and 50.7/50.7 lb (23/23 kg), while Razor Code / X-One Biphase stays at 30 in all three cases. A gap around the same average is therefore a matter of feel. To compare two setups, see our comparison protocol.

✅ 6. Building Your Hybrid in Three Questions

  1. What do you want from the mains? They are the strings that slide. On our index, the stiffer string in the mains gives the same figure as the other way round or a higher one, by 0 to 2 points.
  2. How firm do you want it? Pick a tier, then two strings whose indexes, strung on their own, bracket it.
  3. What does your racquet give? Enter it in the configurator with both strings and both tensions.

❓ FAQ — Hybrid Stringing

What cross string goes with a polyester?

We found no measurement that points to a best cross. A softer cross lowers the index or leaves it unchanged: on an RA 64 racquet at 53 lb (24 kg), Ice Code goes from 31 on its own to 29 with NRG2 crosses.

Is a hybrid more comfortable than a full polyester bed?

On our index, it is never firmer than its polyester on its own when that stiffer polyester is in the mains: 0 to 7 points lower across our 650 measured polyester/multifilament pairs. That does not make it comfortable: none of our six examples reaches the Comfortable tier.

Is a polyester / X-One Biphase hybrid as soft as a full bed of X-One Biphase?

Not with our two examples: with X-One Biphase crosses, Razor Code in the mains gives 30 (Firm) and ALU Power Soft 27 (Standard), against 25 (Standard) for X-One Biphase on its own (RA 64, 53 lb / 24 kg). It depends on the polyester: of our 45 measured polyesters, 3 give 25 or less with this cross. We found no published test that compares the lifespan of a hybrid and a full bed.

Do professional players use hybrid strings?

Babolat wrote in 2020 that 45% of players in the ATP top 20 and 65% "in the WTA" used a hybrid, with no source or season: a brand claim. Roger Federer played gut in the mains and polyester (Luxilon ALU Power Rough) in the crosses, according to Tennis.com in 2009 and The New Yorker in 2014. A professional's frame is often not the retail model, and that setup says nothing about the one that would suit you.

How to Choose Tennis Strings for Clay Courts: Six Criteria That Hold Up

Six measurable criteria (stiffness, spin potential, tension loss, diameter) and the clay myths with no evidence.

✍️ By Pierre Fermanian · Tennis String Advisor • 📅 Last updated 12 November 2026 • ⏱️ 11 min read • 🎾 Strings

Search for the best tennis strings for clay courts and the same three rules come back: drop your tension, go thicker, buy a shaped polyester. They sound like physics. We looked for the evidence behind them, meaning the same string measured on clay and on hard under controlled conditions, and found none.

That does not make the choice arbitrary. It changes the question. Instead of asking which string is made for clay, ask which measurable properties matter for the way you play on clay. Several of them are published with numbers: stiffness, spin potential under fixed lab conditions, tension loss and diameter.

This guide separates what the surface changes from what it does not, walks through six criteria tied to measured data or an established mechanism, and flags the clay claims that have nothing behind them. The goal is a restring decision you can defend with something firmer than a forum thread.

What Clay Changes, and What It Doesn’t

Clay changes the court, not the collision between ball and strings. Stiffness, tension and string friction behave the same on any surface; what clay can alter is the ball that reaches your racquet and how many hits a session puts on the bed. Every criterion below is surface-neutral; clay only changes how much weight each deserves.

The ITF classifies courts by measured pace: a ball is fired at the surface, its speed is measured before and after the bounce, and the speed lost becomes a Court Pace Rating. A rating of 29 or below is Category 1, the slowest class. According to Tennisnerd, European clay courts usually rate between 22 and 25. The same source describes slower, higher-friction courts as giving a higher bounce with more spin. That is a statement about the ball after the bounce, not about your strings.

The most repeated string-specific claim is abrasion: clay particles working into the stringbed and wearing strings faster. It is plausible, and a 2003 US patent states it in its background section. A patent’s background is an assertion, though, not a test. We found no published study that played the same string on clay and on hard under controlled conditions and compared wear or tension.

💡 EXPERT INSIGHT — A “clay string” is a category error

A string has no way of knowing the surface. Its response depends on its stiffness, its tension, the friction between mains and crosses, and the impact it receives. Clay can change that impact, for instance with a slower, higher ball, and so change which property you need most. It cannot make one string intrinsically better on clay than on hard. A ranking of “clay strings” is a ranking of ordinary strings with a label added.

Criterion 1: Stiffness and the Firmness You Will Feel

Stiffness is the measured property that tells you the most about a string. Tennis Warehouse University (TWU) found that spin generally rises with stiffness, and string stiffness is one of the three inputs of felt firmness, alongside frame stiffness and tension.

Stiffness is expressed in pounds of force per inch of stretch (lb/in). In TWU’s String Performance Database, measured at a 51-lb reference tension and a fast swing, values span a wide range: 174.9 lb/in for Weiss Cannon Ultra Cable 1.23 mm, 236.6 lb/in for Babolat RPM Blast 1.35 mm and 280.6 lb/in for Babolat RPM Team 1.30 mm. Compare strings within one database only: the TWU page publishes neither its protocol nor its test dates, and other labs measure differently.

String stiffness alone does not tell you how firm your setup will feel, because the frame and the tension count too. That is what the RCS configurator computes: enter your frame, your string and your tension, and it returns a felt-firmness index and the tier that index falls in. Use the tier to compare setups, not to predict anything about your arm; persistent pain is for a health professional.

Criterion 2: Spin Potential, Measured Rather Than Marketed

TWU publishes a spin potential index for each string under fixed lab conditions. At 51 lb and a fast swing, the highest values belong to Weiss Cannon Ultra Cable 17 (11.9) and Volkl V-Square 16 (11.3). It is a lab ranking valid on any surface, not a clay ranking.

TWU’s 2011 study explains what such an index rewards: spin depends on how far the main strings slide sideways, when they snap back, and how efficiently they return that energy. Snapback is the return of the mains to their resting position during impact. More movement is not automatically better. In those tests, an extremely open 16x10 pattern strung at 30 lb produced the least spin for every string, largely because the strings moved too far and snapped back late; the same pattern at 60 lb produced the most.

Two cautions before you shop by this number. It was measured at one tension and one swing speed, which are not yours. And sample-to-sample spread is real: in the same database, black RPM Blast 1.30 mm scores 4.5 while RPM Blast Orange at the same diameter scores 7.0. A gap of that size should not decide a purchase on its own.

💡 EXPERT INSIGHT — Lower tension is not a spin shortcut

The common logic says lower tension means more string movement, so more spin. TWU’s open-pattern tests show where that logic stops: past a point, extra movement arrives too late to add rotation. If you drop tension for clay because a guide told you to, you can lose spin as easily as you gain depth. Check what the change does to your firmness index before assuming a gain.

Criterion 3: Tension Maintenance and the Dead-String Signal

Every string loses tension, and polyesters lose the most: in TWU’s 2013 tests, polyester lost more than nylon, which lost more than gut. What clay can change is how many hits a session puts on your bed, so judge restringing by symptoms and hours, not by the calendar or the surface.

TWU’s study of how strings “go dead” found that tension falls quickly at first and then stabilises, and that strings differ widely: from the same 62-lb start, the spread between strings reached about 30 lb after 100 hits. It identified two effects that players perceive as “dead”. Lower tension and lower perpendicular stiffness keep the ball on the strings longer and send it deeper, wider or higher. Higher friction between mains and crosses reduces sideways movement and snapback, which cuts spin and makes the bed feel harsher. Both happen at once and partly offset each other.

That gives you a usable diagnostic. Balls flying long, or spin fading while the string looks intact, are consistent with these mechanisms. To see how much firmness a tension drop costs, rerun your setup in the RCS configurator at a lower tension. Temperature also moves tension and stiffness; our article on tennis strings and heat covers it.

Each criterion pulls in its own direction, which is why no single string wins, on clay or anywhere else.

TWU’s own 2011 finding that spin rises with stiffness was stated as a general trend with exceptions, and it named a textured string as one. This table contains another: the least stiff string in it has the highest spin potential. Treat stiffness and spin potential as two separate measurements, and check both.

Criterion 4: Diameter, Read in Millimetres

Compare strings by diameter in millimetres, not by gauge number: the same 1.25 mm is sold as “17” by HEAD and Tecnifibre and as “17L” by Luxilon. Within one string family, TWU’s data does not show the “thinner means more spin” pattern guides repeat.

Look at the RPM Blast rows above. At 1.30 mm and 1.25 mm, stiffness is 232.6 and 233.7 lb/in and spin potential 4.5 and 4.6, which is practically identical. The thickest version, 1.35 mm, scores higher on spin potential (7.3) than both. These are single measurements under one condition: they do not prove that thicker strings spin more, only that this dataset cannot support a gauge rule either way.

Durability is the usual retail argument for thickness. Tennis Warehouse Europe states that thicker gauges are more durable but less elastic and resilient than thinner nylons, and that thinner strings give more spin potential, which the RPM Blast figures above fail to show. Neither statement comes with published measurements, and neither is specific to clay. The full discussion lives in our string gauge guide.

Criterion 5: Durability on Clay, the One Dataset That Is Yours

Faster wear on clay is the most repeated claim and the least measured. Instead of buying on it, measure it: log hours to the first dead-string symptoms and to breakage over two consecutive restrings, and you will hold better clay data than any published source we found.

  • At each restring, note the date, string, diameter and tension.
  • After each session, log the hours played and the surface.
  • Record two events: the first session where balls fly long or spin fades, and the breakage.
  • Change one variable at a time, a thicker diameter of the same string first and then another string, and compare over two restrings.

If you break strings often, that order matters. Keeping the same string isolates the diameter, and your firmness index tells you what the change costs in feel.

Criterion 6: Tension, Where No Surface Rule Survives

“Drop one to two kilograms on clay” is advice, not a measured result. The best-known clay-court player did not follow it: Rafael Nadal said he always played at the same tension, 25 kg in normal conditions and 25.5 kg at altitude.

A second account, from his stringing team, agrees. Joshua Newton, who led Babolat’s stringing team at the 2018 French Open, described RPM Blast 1.35 mm in mains and crosses, usually at 25 kg and occasionally 25.5 kg, as reported by tennisplayer.net. Treat that as a data point about his setup, not a target for yours: a tour player’s racquet cannot be assumed to match the retail model, and his tension worked with his frame, his string and his swing.

What does transfer is a method: hold felt firmness constant whenever something else changes. If you switch strings for clay, enter both setups in the RCS configurator and find the tension that returns your previous firmness index, then adjust one step at a time. Our string tension guide covers the rest.

Clay Claims and the Evidence Behind Them

Of the five claims you will meet most often, none rests on a controlled clay-versus-hard comparison. Two are unverified, two are general string facts relabelled as clay advice, and one is contradicted in a lab test.

FAQ: Tennis Strings and Clay Courts

Do I need a different string for clay courts?

No evidence shows a string performing better on clay than on hard. Clay changes the ball you receive and how many hits a session puts on your strings, not the stringbed’s properties. If your setup works, keep it, and re-weight the criteria (spin potential, tension maintenance, durability) for how you actually play on clay.

Should I lower my string tension for clay?

Not by rule. “Drop one to two kilograms” is widespread advice with no controlled comparison behind it, and Rafael Nadal said he played at 25 kg in all normal conditions. If you change anything for clay, keep your felt-firmness index constant in the RCS configurator, then adjust in small steps.

Do strings wear out faster on clay?

Possibly, but nobody has shown it under controlled conditions. The claim appears in the background section of a 2003 US patent, not in a published test. The reliable answer is your own log: hours to breakage and to the first loss of spin or control, over two restrings played on clay.

Is 16 or 17 gauge better for clay?

Compare millimetres, because labels vary: 1.25 mm is sold as both 17 and 17L. In TWU’s lab data, RPM Blast at 1.30 mm and 1.25 mm shows nearly identical stiffness and spin potential. Thicker strings are said to last longer, but that is a retailer recommendation, not a clay-specific measurement.

What are the best tennis strings for clay courts?

No evidence-based clay ranking exists, and we don’t publish one. What exists are lab values valid on any surface: at 51 lb and a fast swing, TWU’s highest spin potential scores are Weiss Cannon Ultra Cable 17 (11.9) and Volkl V-Square 16 (11.3). Weigh them against tension loss and felt firmness.

Why do my strings feel dead after a few clay sessions?

TWU identified two causes. Tension and perpendicular stiffness drop, so the ball stays on the strings longer and flies deeper or higher. Friction between mains and crosses rises, so snapback and spin fall. Both can happen while the string looks intact: restring on those symptoms, whatever the surface.

Conclusion: Same Criteria, Honest Weighting

Clay does not call for a different string. It calls for ranking the same measurable criteria honestly for your game, and for measuring your own wear instead of trusting folklore.

🎾 Heavy-topspin baseliner

Shortlist by measured spin potential, then drop any string whose tension loss means restringing before the spin pays off.

✂️ Frequent string breaker

Keep your string and test a thicker diameter of it over two restrings before changing string.

🛋️ Comfort-first player

Choose by firmness tier in the RCS configurator, not by material labels. Persistent pain belongs with a health professional.

🔁 Moving from hard to clay with a setup you like

Change nothing, log your hours, and let your own data decide the next restring.

Tennis String Tension Guide: How Many Pounds Should You Really String At?

What tension really changes, why your string bed is looser than you asked for, and what is actually known about a gap between mains and crosses.

📅 August 14, 2026 · updated November 12, 2026 • ⏱️ 12 min read • 🎾 Equipment

There is no universally right tension: tension sets how firm your string bed is, and that firmness is what you are really choosing. As for a gap between mains and crosses, no primary source gives a number: the "+4 lb on the crosses" you will read about comes from a forum, and the opposite habit exists too.

  • The tension you ask for is not the tension in your string bed: right after stringing it is about 30% lower, 40% with polyester (Cross and Bower, 2001).
  • Each kg (about 2.2 lb) less takes 0.5 point off your setup's RCS index: with the catalogue's median polyester offered in 1.25 mm, on an RA 64 racquet, the setup moves from the Standard band to Firm from 50.7 lb (23 kg).
  • Pro tensions do not transfer: they vary between sources and are strung in pro stock racquets.

This guide separates what has been measured, what is stringers' habit and what nobody has measured, then gives you a method: pick a firmness, then the string and tension that get you there. Every tension is given in pounds first, kilograms in brackets.

📐 First, the units: pounds and kilos

Most English-speaking stringers work in pounds; much of the technical literature and most European stringers use kilograms. One kilogram equals 2.2046 lb, so 1 kg is a shade over 2 lb.

⚗️ 1. What tension changes: string bed firmness

Lower tension makes the string bed softer, and a softer bed sends the ball back faster for the same swing. Higher tension does the opposite: the ball comes off slower, which players call control.

The mechanism is measured. According to Tennis Warehouse University (TWU), all strings return about 95% of the energy they take in, while the ball loses 45 to 47% of the energy that goes into deforming it. The softer the bed, the bigger its share of the deformation and the less energy the ball wastes: that is the whole secret of "power" from low tension.

A string's stiffness (in lb/in, the force that stretches it one inch in a fast impact) rises with tension. Measured by TWU at 30 and then 60 lb, it goes from 233 to 304 lb/in for Babolat RPM Blast 16, and from 148 to 190 lb/in for Tecnifibre X-One Biphase 16.

💡

EXPERT NOTE

A string does not "give" power: it makes the ball lose less. For TWU, a "less powerful" string simply takes in less energy than a softer one at impact. Tension and string stiffness therefore pull the same lever, string bed firmness, which is why the RCS index combines them.

1.1 What about spin?

With polyester, the mains slide over the crosses on impact, then snap back and brush the ball upward: that is snapback. The role of tension in it is not established: in TWU's string pattern study, it remains inconclusive.

Another TWU experiment even contradicts the "lower means more spin" idea: on a bed with every other cross removed, crosses at 60 lb rather than 30 lb gave almost 40% more spin, by holding the mains back. That rig is nothing like a 16 × 19; it only shows that no simple rule links tension and spin.

📉 2. The tension you ask for is not the tension in your string bed

The number you give your stringer is a pull tension, applied string by string by the machine. Right after stringing the bed is already well below it, and it keeps relaxing: fast at first, slowly afterwards.

Loss is fastest at stringing, over the following hours and on the first hits; after that it becomes almost imperceptible, but it adds up (TWU). On racquets, over 200 hours, Cross and Bower could not separate the loss caused by hitting from the string's natural relaxation.

2.1 What about string families?

Across every protocol, polyester loses more than nylon, which loses more than gut; the numbers depend on the protocol. In TWU's database (51 lb, "Fast" speed), the median loss, calculated by us, is 41.2% for 483 polyesters, 20.5% for 230 nylons and 14.9% for 18 guts. TWU does not publish the exact protocol, and it is not a "24-hour" loss: no verifiable source gives one per family.

💡

EXPERT NOTE

What you feel on court is the stiffness of the bed after stringing and relaxation, not the tension you asked for. To compare two tensions, keep the same stringer, the same machine and the same delay between stringing and playing; otherwise you are comparing protocols, not tensions.

When the frame changes too (head size, string pattern), the same reference tension no longer gives the same firmness: see how to compare two string setups.

↔️ 3. Mains and crosses: should the tensions differ?

No primary source, whether manufacturer, laboratory or federation, sets a tension gap between mains and crosses. The "+4 lb on the crosses" is a stringers' habit relayed by forums; the opposite habit, looser crosses, exists too, with an equally physical logic.

What has been measured is the gap stringing creates by itself. At equal pull tension, Cross and Bower found with a Stringmeter crosses up to 10 kg (22 lb) looser than the mains depending on machine and technique, and mains that stay tighter after months of play. The main cause is friction: each cross is pulled through mains already in place, and its tension changes from one end to the other (28 kg at one end, about 20 kg at the other in one of their examples).

3.1 Two habits, two logics

Habit Stated logic What supports it What is missing
Tighter crosses ("+4 lb", 1.8 kg)Make up for tension lost to friction while stringingThe crosses' deficit is measured (Cross and Bower, 2001)No measurement of the resulting bed; the figure comes from a forum (2011) and a rumour (Tennis.com, 2024)
Looser crosses (up to 2 kg, about 4.4 lb)Being shorter, crosses are stiffer at equal tensionMechanism described by Cross and Bower; habit described by Tennis.com and RacketpediaNo measurement; 2 kg cap unsourced

Same observation, opposite conclusions: without a measurement of the resulting bed, neither is a rule. In a hybrid (two different strings), the specialist press describes the habit of stringing the softer string tighter, wherever it sits: a habit, not a measurement.

The RCS index uses the average of mains and crosses when the same string goes in both. With Head Lynx Tour on an RA 64 racquet, 52.9/48.5 lb (24/22 kg), 50.7/50.7 lb (23/23 kg) or 48.5 lb mains with crosses 4 lb higher all give 31. A gap around the same average leaves the index unchanged; you judge it by feel.

💡

EXPERT NOTE

The gap you ask for is not the gap you get. If crosses already come out looser than mains at equal pull tension, "+4 lb" may simply shrink that gap without reversing it; nobody has published the measurement that would settle it. To test it, keep the same average tension and have both racquets strung on the same day by the same stringer.

🎯 4. Choosing a tension: start from firmness

No verifiable source sets a tension by playing level. What can be calculated is how firm your setup is: the RCS index combines the racquet's stiffness (its RA), the string's stiffness and the tension. Our configurator computes it for your own setup.

Each kg (about 2.2 lb) less removes 0.5 point; 10 lb/in less string stiffness, about 1.1 points; one RA point, about 0.3 point. Bands: under 20, Very comfortable; 20 to 24, Comfortable; 25 to 29, Standard; 30 to 34, Firm; 35 and above, Very firm.

The index does not yet distinguish gauges and uses the stiffest measured one: for Head Lynx Tour, the 1.30 mm (228.6 lb/in), while TWU measures the 1.25 mm at 217.7 lb/in, about one index point apart.

At the site's reference setup (RA 64, 52.9 lb / 24 kg), the median polyester sits in the Firm band, index 30, two points below the sensitive-arm alert threshold (32). 💡

EXPERT NOTE

Changing string moves the index much faster than changing tension. At 52.9 lb (24 kg) on an RA 64 racquet, going from Head Lynx Tour to Luxilon 4G Soft removes one point, the same as 2 kg (4.4 lb) less; going to Yonex Poly Tour Air removes eight, the equivalent of 16 kg (35 lb). ✅ Where to start

The right tension is the one that gives your setup the firmness you want: change one thing at a time and check the index.

  • Your balls land short for the same swing: lower the tension, or pick a less stiff string, which moves the index faster.
  • Your balls fly too fast: raise the tension and watch which band your setup reaches.
  • You are testing a mains/crosses gap: same average tension, same stringer; no numerical rule exists, your feel decides.
  • You are changing string: compare the two setups' indices, not their tensions.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Should I string my crosses tighter than my mains?

No primary source says so. The "+4 lb on the crosses" comes from a forum, and the opposite habit exists too. What is measured: at equal pull tension, crosses already come out looser than mains, by up to 10 kg (22 lb) depending on machine and technique (Cross and Bower, 2001).

What tension for a 1.25 mm (17 gauge) polyester?

No source sets a tension by gauge. On an RA 64 racquet, the catalogue's median polyester offered in 1.25 mm gives an RCS index of 28 at 44 lb (20 kg) and 31 at 57.3 lb (26 kg): Standard band up to 48.5 lb (22 kg), Firm from 50.7 lb (23 kg).

How much tension do strings lose right after stringing?

On racquets, about 30% of the pull tension, about 40% with polyester (Cross and Bower, 2001). On a single string tensioned at 62 lb, TWU measured up to 15 lb lost in one minute. No verifiable source gives a "24-hour" loss per string family.

Why do two racquets strung at the same tension feel different?

Because the tension you ask for is not the tension in the bed: stringer, machine, stringing method and the delay before play all change it. In a Racketpedia test, one racquet strung at 52.9 lb (24 kg) measured 51.6 to 53.6 lb (23.4 to 24.3 kg) depending on the method.

Should I copy a pro's tension?

No. Published pro tensions vary between sources, name no stringer and apply to pro stock racquets, often different from the retail model sold under the same name. They tell you nothing about what suits your swing.

Does lower tension give more power?

Yes, for the same swing. Strings return about 95% of the energy they take in, while the ball loses 45 to 47% (TWU). A softer bed takes a bigger share of the deformation, so the ball wastes less energy and comes off faster.

Tennis String Gauge: Does Thinner Really Play Better?

Spin, power, feel, durability — a tenth of a millimetre moves all four at once. Here is how the gauge system works, what each step actually costs you, and how diameter interacts with tension.

📅 August 14, 2026 • ⏱️ 10 min read • 🎾 String Guide

Most players spend weeks agonising over which string to buy and what tension to ask for, then let the stringer pick the gauge by default. That is the one spec chosen at random — and it is not a rounding error. Keep the same string and the same tension, drop from 1.30 mm to 1.25 mm, and you change how the ball grips, how much energy comes back, what the impact feels like, and how many sessions you get before it snaps.

This guide covers what gauge means, the two numbering systems you will run into on a package, what each of the four trade-offs really buys you, and how to pick a diameter you can live with.

🔍 What "gauge" actually means

Gauge is simply the diameter of the string. Thinner string is more elastic, bites into the ball more, and plays livelier. Thicker string is more durable and more controlled. In practice, tennis strings run from roughly 1.10 mm to 1.40 mm — a spread of three tenths of a millimetre that covers everything from a touch player's 18 gauge to a heavy hitter's 15.

Two numbering systems coexist. Europe prints the diameter in millimetres. The United States uses a gauge number that runs backwards: the higher the number, the thinner the string. So 18 gauge is thinner than 16 gauge, which trips up nearly everyone the first time. The suffix "L" — as in 16L or 15L — stands for Light and marks a half step thinner than the base number.

🎾 Expert insight: gauge numbering is not standardised across brands. One company's "16" can measure the same as another company's "16L". Always compare the millimetre figure printed on the package — that is the only number that means the same thing everywhere.

📊 Gauge to millimetre conversion chart

US gauge Approx. diameter Profile & typical use
15 ≈ 1.35 – 1.40 mm Very thick. Maximum durability for chronic string breakers.
15L ≈ 1.33 – 1.35 mm Thick. Nearly the same lifespan, marginally livelier.
16 ≈ 1.28 – 1.32 mm The default reference. Balanced, ideal for regular breakers.
16L ≈ 1.23 – 1.28 mm Versatile. Splits the difference between power and control.
17 ≈ 1.20 – 1.25 mm Thin. More power, feel and comfort; shorter lifespan.
17L ≈ 1.18 – 1.20 mm Very thin. Heightened touch and feedback.
18 ≈ 1.15 – 1.20 mm The thinnest in common circulation. Maximum spin and touch, fragile.

Note how the ranges overlap. A 17 at 1.25 mm and a 16L at 1.23 mm are effectively the same string bed, sold under different labels. That overlap is exactly why the millimetre figure beats the gauge number when you compare two products side by side in our string database.

⚙️ The four things gauge changes in your game

Spin

A thinner string sinks further into the felt and grips the ball harder. It also moves more freely inside the string bed and returns to position faster — the snapback effect — and that snapback is what launches the ball with rotation. The honest caveat: gauge on its own is a subtle lever. Your technique and the string type you choose both matter more than the tenth of a millimetre. Dropping a gauge will not fix a flat swing path.

Power

Thinner means more elastic. The string deforms further on impact and gives more of that energy back to the ball. A thicker string is stiffer, deforms less, and returns a more measured, more predictable response. If you already generate plenty of pace yourself, the extra return from a thin gauge can push balls long; if you are looking for free depth, it works in your favour.

Feel and control

Thick gauge gives a duller, muted impact and a response you can count on shot after shot. Thin gauge is sharper and more reactive — you feel more of what is happening at contact — but it can be less stable on flat drives, where the string bed has less structure to push back with.

Durability

This is the central trade-off, and there is no clever way around it. At identical string type and tension, a thinner string notches faster where the mains and crosses saw against each other, and it breaks sooner. Everything you gain in spin, power and feel is paid for in restringing frequency.

Expert insight — comfort is mostly about type, not diameter. A thin gauge is not automatically softer on the arm. Comfort depends far more on what the string is made of (a stiff polyester versus a multifilament or natural gut) than on how thick it is. If your elbow is talking to you, change the material first and treat gauge as the fine adjustment afterwards.

🔧 Gauge and tension: the interaction to understand

A thicker string holds its tension longer, because it stretches less under the same load. At an identical stringing tension it also plays relatively firmer — the number on the machine is the same, the bed is not.

There is no universal formula for recalculating tension when you change gauge. Do not trust a conversion table that promises one. Judge it on ball flight instead: if the ball is flying long after a change, come down; if it is dropping short, go up. Move in steps of about 2 lbs (1 kg) and give each setting a full session before deciding. Moving substantially thicker is the one case where taking roughly 2 lbs (1 kg) off is a reasonable starting point, precisely because the thicker bed plays firmer at the same number.

🎯 Which gauge for which player

You break strings often

Gauge 15 to 16 (1.28 – 1.40 mm), or a hybrid setup using a thick poly in the mains where the wear happens.

All-court, no strong preference

Gauge 16 or 17 (1.25 – 1.30 mm). This is the default that suits the largest number of players.

Maximum spin and touch

Gauge 17 to 18 (1.15 – 1.25 mm), ideally a shaped profile. Accept that you will be restringing more often.

Sensitive arm

Pick a soft string type first — that decision does most of the work — then fine-tune the gauge within it.

The practical rule that comes out of all this: the right gauge is the thinnest one you can use without breaking strings more often than you are willing to pay for. Start one step thinner than you think, and step back up if the restringing bill becomes the dominant cost of your season.

🏆 What the pros play

Player Gauge Rationale
🇪🇸 Rafael Nadal ~1.35 mm (gauge 15) Maximum durability under an extreme spin workload.
🇪🇸 Carlos Alcaraz ~1.30 mm (gauge 16) Control inside a very powerful game.
🇨🇭 Roger Federer Hybrid: 1.30 mm / 1.25 mm Natural gut 1.30 mm in the mains, poly 1.25 mm in the crosses.

Two things stand out. First, none of them plays the thinnest gauge available — at that level, a string that breaks mid-rally costs more than the spin it buys. Second, Federer's hybrid shows the sophisticated move: rather than one compromise diameter throughout, use a thicker, softer string in the mains for feel and a thinner poly in the crosses. ✅ The takeaway

Gauge is the quiet slider between performance and longevity. Go thinner and you gain bite, feel and free power; go thicker and you gain control, tension stability and weeks of playing life. Neither end is correct in the abstract — the correct answer is the thinnest string your breakage rate tolerates.

Three habits will keep you out of trouble. Compare millimetres, never gauge numbers, when you shop across brands. Change one variable at a time, so you know what caused what. And when you do change gauge, re-evaluate tension by ball flight rather than by formula, in steps of about 2 lbs (1 kg). If you want more articles in this series, the full library sits in our blog.

One tenth of a millimetre will not rescue a bad string choice, but it will make a good one noticeably better — or quietly ruin it. It is the cheapest experiment in your bag: the next restring costs the same whichever number you ask for.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Which string gauge should I use by default?

Gauge 16 or 17 (1.25 – 1.30 mm) is the best compromise for the large majority of players. Start at 17 for the extra feel and spin, and move up to 16 if you find yourself breaking strings faster than you want to pay for.

16 vs 17 gauge: what is the real difference?

In millimetres, roughly 1.30 mm versus 1.25 mm. The 17 (1.25 mm) gives you more spin, more touch and more power. The 16 (1.30 mm) gives you more control, better durability and better tension maintenance. Same string, same tension, opposite ends of the same trade-off.

Do I need to change tension when I change gauge?

There is no universal rule. A thicker string plays firmer at the same number and holds tension longer, so if you move substantially thicker, dropping about 2 lbs (1 kg) is a sensible starting point. Then adjust by ball flight, not by table.

Is thinner string worse for my elbow?

Not by itself. Arm comfort is driven far more by string material — a stiff polyester versus a multifilament or natural gut — than by diameter. Choose a comfortable string type first, then set the gauge inside it, and check the firmness of the whole setup in the configurator before you string.

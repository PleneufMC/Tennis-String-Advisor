Polyester vs Multifilament: The Real Criteria Nobody Tells You About

"Poly is for good players only." You hear it in every club. But if the trampoline feel of multifilament drives you crazy, that advice is answering the wrong question — because your rating was never the criterion.

📅 August 14, 2026 • ⏱️ 11 min read • 🎾 String Guide

It is the most common between-matches question at any club: should you play polyester or multifilament? The stock answer — reserve poly for the club elite — is misleading. It confuses a ranking number with what actually determines the right string: your swing, your breakage rate, the state of your arm, and the feel you are chasing.

A note on vocabulary before we start, because it causes endless confusion. "Poly", "polyester" and "monofilament" all mean the same product: a single-strand polyester string. The English-speaking market says "poly"; the French say "mono". Same thing. Whenever you read "poly" below, that is what we mean.

The short version

  • The criterion is not your rating, but a trio: swing speed and path, breakage rate, and arm health.
  • Polyester: control, spin and durability — but stiff. String it low (most polys perform best around 35–40 lbs / 16–18 kg) and replace it often.
  • Multifilament: comfort, power and forgiveness. The default for sensitive arms, juniors and shorter swings.
  • Hybrid (poly mains, multi or gut crosses): the best compromise between control and comfort.

Two materials, two opposite sets of physics

Before choosing, understand that you are not comparing two versions of the same object. You are comparing two families with opposing physical properties. Benoît Mauguin, the stringer for France's Davis Cup team, makes this point in an interview with the French Tennis Federation: poly and multi differ so much in both material and structure that they should not even be tensioned the same way.

Polyester (monofilament)

A polyester string is a single solid strand, usually polyester or co-polyester. It is now the dominant material on the ATP and WTA tours, and a very common choice among club competitors. That single mass transmits impact shock straight through to the hand, which is where its stiffness and firm feel come from. In exchange it delivers excellent control, high durability, and outstanding spin access: the smooth surface lets the mains slide sideways under the ball and then snap back into place, and that snapback is what puts rotation on the ball.

Multifilament

A multifilament is built from hundreds — sometimes thousands — of microfibres, often nylon, twisted or braided together and wrapped in a protective coating. The bundle structure is an attempt to imitate natural gut: on impact each fibre shifts slightly and disperses the energy. The result is a soft, comfortable, powerful string that absorbs vibration well. Its limits: it frays, it breaks quickly under heavy hitters, and it gives up control and spin compared to a poly.

Natural gut

Natural gut remains the absolute benchmark for comfort, power and tension maintenance. Its price and its sensitivity to moisture keep it a minority choice, but it is worth knowing as a reference point — and it matters in hybrid setups, which we come back to below.

Where the "poly is for good players" myth comes from

The idea rests on three real observations that get misread. First, professionals play poly almost universally, so by imitation poly became a badge of standard. Second, poly genuinely requires racquet head speed: without a fast, upward swing capable of moving the mains and letting them snap back, polyester never "works" — it just sits there being stiff and harsh. Third, poly can hurt your arm when it is strung too tight, which is exactly what amateurs do when they carry their multifilament tension straight over.

None of those three things reduces to a ranking number. Asked about it by the French federation, Mauguin is blunt: he sees modest club players who destroy string beds and high-level competitors who almost never break. There is no rule. Tennis is too heterogeneous for one: a slight player who uses a lot of wrist has nothing in common, string-wise, with a big hitter who drives flat.

The question is not "am I good enough for poly?" It is "does my game make poly work, and can my arm take it?"

One historical detail sharpens the point: the rise of poly pushed manufacturers toward more forgiving frames — larger heads, more open string patterns — precisely to absorb polys that were routinely strung too tight. The industry adapted to poly becoming the norm. It did not make poly the reward for reaching a level.

The other side: comfort advocates, and who pays them

Not every expert lands in the same place, and that is worth hearing. Plenty of well-followed coaches and content creators argue for multifilament, emphasising comfort and injury prevention. That argument is real and important, especially for sensitive arms — nothing in this article softens it.

One nuance deserves to stay in view: some of those voices are brand ambassadors, often for multifilament manufacturers. Tecnifibre, the world leader in multifilament, claims — study in hand — that one of its multifilaments fatigues the arm around 22% less than a polyester. There is nothing illegitimate about that, but it is a good reason to judge each piece of advice on its merits rather than on the profile of whoever is giving it. For a heavy hitter with a healthy arm and a fast swing, the extra comfort of multi is usually paid for in control and spin. Only you can weigh that trade against your own body and your own game.

Who should play polyester

Poly is a good choice if several of these describe you:

  • You hit hard, with a fast upward swing. You have the racquet head speed to exploit snapback and the spin poly offers.
  • You break multifilament or gut regularly. Poly's durability starts paying for itself.
  • You are chasing control and precision. You want to pin the ball down and place it, and you read the court better through a firm bed with a low launch angle.
  • The elastic feel of multi bothers you: balls sailing long, vague placement, a mushy response on the return. That is exactly the profile that gains from switching.
  • You accept restringing on a schedule, because poly dies well before it breaks.

Three conditions come with it: a healthy arm, a racquet on the heavier side — roughly above 285 g unstrung, since poly in a very light frame generates unpleasant vibration — and a moderate tension.

Who should play multifilament

Multifilament remains the better choice if:

  • You are a beginner or improving intermediate still building technique. A soft, forgiving bed protects that learning phase.
  • Your swing is shorter or slower and you want free depth and power.
  • Your arm is sensitive — tennis elbow, tendinitis — or you are coming back from injury. Softness limits the shock load.
  • You are a junior. Stiff poly has no business in a young player's frame; shoulder injuries in juniors strung too early on very stiff polyester are reported regularly.
  • You do not break strings, want longer intervals between restrings, and value tension that holds.

🩺 On arm pain, one rule beats all the others. If you already have elbow or shoulder pain, no string, tension or hybrid combination is a treatment. Equipment can stop making a problem worse; it does not fix one that has settled in. See a health professional, and treat any softer setup as damage limitation rather than a cure.

Where the debate actually lives: solid intermediate to strong competitor

The real battleground is the band that runs from the consistent intermediate who can hold a game plan up to the experienced club competitor. (In the French ranking system, where this debate is usually framed, that is roughly the stretch from 30/1 up to 15/2.) At those levels you control zones and tempo, and you generally have enough racquet head speed to make a polyester work. The idea that poly would be "too much" for you does not hold: what matters is your swing and your arm, not the rung you occupy.

A story that circulates on player forums illustrates the opposite trap perfectly. A strong club player with a flat game and variations tries a demo racquet strung with a thin poly (1.24 mm) at around 53–55 lbs (24–25 kg). The feel is exceptional: control, power, comfort. He buys the frame, has it strung with his usual multifilament — and loses everything. He cannot generate spin, and the ball has neither power nor depth. The racquet had nothing to do with it. If you dislike multifilament, you are probably living the same experiment in reverse.

The explanation is mechanical. A flat game with variations needs a predictable, directional bed with a low launch angle. Multifilament returns a lot of energy and sends the ball up and out, which produces exactly the long balls and vague placement that frustrate this player. Poly pins the ball down and hands the trajectory back to you, while still offering the snapback your heavier topspin drives need.

For a player in that band who dislikes multi, polyester is not merely permitted — it is probably the right answer. The one real reason to stay away is arm pain, and that is a health question, not a level question.

Softening poly without giving up control

If poly tempts you but the arm worries you, several levers exist before you abandon the control it gives.

Tension: the number one amateur mistake

You do not string a poly like a multi. For most polyesters the sweet spot sits around 35–40 lbs (16–18 kg); there is little reason to go far above it. The practical rule: string a poly roughly 10% lower than you would a multifilament. And do not exceed roughly 53–55 lbs (24–25 kg) in polyester, or you lose the qualities you came for and start punishing your arm. For scale, the tour spans an enormous range — Jannik Sinner strings around 62 lbs (28 kg) while Adrian Mannarino sometimes drops under 22 lbs (10 kg) — but those are professional extremes, not templates to copy.

Gauge

A thinner gauge (1.20 to 1.25 mm) gives more feel and more spin bite. Go thicker only if you are breaking too fast.

The "soft poly" question

The market is now full of soft co-polys whose published stiffness approaches that of some multifilaments. Be careful: "soft" does not mean durably comfortable. These strings soften thanks to elastomers, and the effect is short-lived — after roughly 6 to 12 hours of play the fibres set, the bed locks up, and the initial softness is gone. Hence the golden rule: do not keep a polyester beyond about 15 hours of play, even unbroken. A dead poly no longer controls anything and becomes genuinely traumatic for the arm.

Measure where your setup actually sits

It is nearly impossible to tell by eye whether your setup leans too far toward stiffness (a risk for the arm) or too far toward softness (a loss of control). What about hybrid stringing?

A hybrid setup uses two different strings for the mains (vertical) and the crosses (horizontal). The most common club combination — polyester in the mains, multifilament or gut in the crosses — is a genuinely good compromise: the poly mains dominate the response and supply control, spin and durability, while the softer crosses take the edge off impact. For a player in the intermediate-to-competitor band who wants poly's control without punishing the elbow, this is often the best available balance.

The reverse configuration — Roger Federer's famous gut mains, poly crosses — prioritises touch with a hint of control, but it wears out fast for heavy topspin players.

The three-question method

Instead of consulting your ranking, answer these three honestly.

  1. Do I break strings often?
    Yes → polyester's durability justifies itself. No → multifilament stays relevant and more comfortable.
  2. Is my swing fast and upward?
    Yes → you will exploit poly's snapback and spin. No → poly will never "work"; it will just feel stiff, and multi will give you far more return.
  3. Do I have arm or elbow pain?
    Yes → multifilament or gut first, or a hybrid / soft poly strung low. No → poly is safe to explore.

Tick "breaks strings + fast swing + healthy arm" and polyester is right for you, whatever your rating says. If pain appears, that — not your level — is what should push you back toward softness.

Conclusion

"Poly is for good players only" is a shortcut that survives because it sounds sensible, but it misses what matters. What decides your string is your swing, your breakage rate, the state of your arm and the feel you want — not a line on a ranking list. Across the whole intermediate-to-competitor band, a player who swings fast, wants to direct the ball, and finds multifilament too soft and too springy has every reason to choose polyester. It is a coherent choice, an effective one, and probably the one that gives them their best sensations back.

One principle every stringer repeats: try it and write it down. At identical gauge and tension, two poly references can feel like opposites. Keeping a string log — what you mounted, at what tension, and how it felt — remains the most reliable way to converge on your own setting. The right string is not the one your level supposedly entitles you to. It is the one that fits your game and protects your arm. More equipment guides are collected in the Tennis String Advisor blog.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Is polyester really only for advanced players?

No. The criterion is not your rating but a combination of three factors: swing speed and path, breakage rate, and arm health. A solid intermediate with a fast swing exploits polyester perfectly well, while a higher-rated player with a sensitive arm may be better off in multifilament. France's federation stringer makes the same point: there is no rule tied to level.

At what level does polyester start to make sense?

There is no official threshold. The debate really lives in the band running from the consistent intermediate to the experienced club competitor. At those levels you control zones and tempo and usually have enough racquet head speed to make the string work. The conditions: a healthy arm, a suitable racquet (rather above 285 g unstrung), a moderate tension, and disciplined restringing.

Why do I dislike multifilament?

It is a common reaction among control-oriented players. Multifilament is elastic: it returns a lot of energy and sends the ball out at a high launch angle. The result is balls that fly long, less precise placement, and a soft or springy sensation. A player who hits hard and wants to direct the ball usually feels far more confident with the stiffness and lower launch angle of a polyester.

What tension should I use for polyester?

Lower than for multifilament — roughly 10% less. Most polyesters perform best around 35–40 lbs (16–18 kg). Beyond about 53–55 lbs (24–25 kg) a poly loses its qualities and turns aggressive on the arm. Start in the middle of the manufacturer's recommended range for your frame, then go down for power and comfort or up for control.

Poly, hybrid or multi to protect the arm?

If you feel discomfort, the safest options are multifilament or natural gut. To keep poly's control while sparing the arm, there are two routes: a hybrid setup (polyester mains, multifilament or gut crosses) or a soft polyester strung low. Be aware that a soft poly's comfort is short-lived and disappears after a few hours of play, which makes disciplined restringing essential. If pain has already settled in, do not expect equipment alone to solve it.

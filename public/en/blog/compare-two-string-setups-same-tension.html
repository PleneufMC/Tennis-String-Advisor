Same String Tension, Different Head Size: How to Compare Two Setups

The tension you ask for sets a machine. What you can compare from one racquet to the next is stringbed stiffness, measured twice the same way.

📅 November 13, 2026 • ⏱️ 9 min read • 🎾 Strings

🎯 The short version

  • The tension you ask for is a machine setting, not a property of the stringbed.
  • Measured: same frame, same reference tension, three strings: 69, 78 and 80 stiffness units, a 16% spread.
  • Not measured: the effect of head size and string pattern. We know the direction, not the scale.
  • To compare: same instrument, same delay after stringing and, if you can, the same frame.
  • Our RCS index compares two strings or two tensions on one frame; it sees neither head size nor pattern.

You ask for 53 lb (24 kg) on your 98, your hitting partner asks for 53 lb on a 105, and the two racquets do not answer the same way. Or you switch frames, keep "your" tension, and the feel is gone. The first instinct is to blame the stringer. The usual cause lies elsewhere: the number you give at the counter sets a machine; it does not describe the stringbed you get back.

This article settles three points. What the reference tension guarantees, and what it does not, once the frame changes. What the instruments that claim to read "the tension" of a strung racquet are worth. What our firmness index can compare between two frames, and what it cannot see.

You will leave with a protocol: what to measure, when, and with what. The gap between the tension you ask for and the tension in the stringbed is covered in our string tension guide; we only come back to it here to place it.

🎯 1. Why the Same Tension Does Not Give the Same Firmness From One Racquet to the Next

Because the tension you ask for sets the pulling force of the machine, string after string, and nothing else. The firmness you feel is the stiffness of the strung bed: the force it puts up against being pushed in. It also depends on the string, on how long and how many the strings are, on the stringing method and on the time elapsed: two racquets "at 53 lb" share a setting, not a result.

Tennis Warehouse University (TWU) puts it in one sentence: "individual string stiffness is not the same as stringbed stiffness". Stringbed stiffness also depends on string length (head size and shape), on the number of strings (the pattern) and on the tension already lost. TWU puts no figure on any of these factors.

The gap between the reference tension and the tension in the stringbed (about 30% lower right after stringing, on five nylon-strung racquets, according to Cross and Bower) is covered in the guide. The question here is the next one: at the same reference tension, what moves the result?

What changes, at the same reference tension Published spread Source and limits

The first result is the clearest. On a Wilson Pro Staff 6.1 95 (16×18) and a constant-pull machine, the bed comes off at 69 units with a "poly" monofilament (Tourna Poly Big Hitter 16), 78 with the control nylon (Prince Synthetic Gut Original Gold 16) and 80 with a multifilament (Head FiberGel Power 16). The next day, without a single ball hit: 65, 71 and 73.

📐 2. Head Size and String Pattern: The Direction Is Known, the Scale Is Not

At the same reference tension, a larger head tends to give a softer bed, because its strings are longer; a more open pattern pulls the same way. The direction of both effects is established. No published measurement we could open isolates them, so there is no defensible rule of the "so many pounds per square inch" kind.

TWU lists string length and string count among what determines stringbed stiffness; Cross and Bower write that a deflection reading moves with them too, and not with tension alone. Stringers say it too (larger head, higher reference tension for the same firmness), without a measurement: a trade signal, not a data point.

💡 Expert point — why string length matters. A string under tension resists being pushed in proportion to its tension and in inverse proportion to its length: at equal tension, a longer string gives more. On a real stringbed the strings cross, their length changes from the centre to the edges, and the pattern often changes with head size. The direction is safe; the size of the effect is still to be measured.

Move from a 98 to a 105 with "your" tension, and you keep the setting, not the stringbed. See also string pattern and string gauge, which TWU lists among the same factors.

🔧 3. How to Measure Stringbed Firmness: What the Instruments Are Worth

No instrument reads the reference tension back: each measures something else, in its own unit. All of them can track one racquet over time. For comparing two different racquets, we found no independent validation of any consumer instrument.

Instrument What it measures, in what Published accuracy Defensible use
Two-prong gauge (Stringmeter type) Local tension of one string, in lb or kg About 2 lb (1 kg) on gut and nylon, not on Kevlar (Cross and Bower) Track one racquet
Deflection device (RDC type) How far the bed gives under a load, in device-specific units Not stated in the tests we read Track one racquet; compare two strings on the same frame
Frequency device (ERT 300) Stringbed frequency converted to a "DT" stiffness, in kp/cm (kilogram-force per centimetre) ±1 kp/cm, range 20 to 65 (manual) Track one racquet; across frames, on the manual's word only
Sound app (RacquetTune) Stringbed frequency, in Hz, converted to tension Changes of 0.2 lb (0.1 kg), according to the publisher Track one racquet

Cross and Bower are blunt: a deflection reading moves with tension, and also with string length and string count. Frequency is the finest tool for tracking one racquet: a 1% change in tension is easy to measure, and 10% less tension means 5% less frequency (from 600 to 570 Hz, for instance).

The ERT 300 manual claims the device accounts on its own for racquet size, pattern and string; yet its conversion disc to a tension splits head sizes into three classes. That is the manufacturer's word.

💡 Expert point — a frequency does not travel from one head to another. At equal tension and string, a 98 in² head vibrates about 3.5% higher than a 105 in² (our calculation, from Cross's formula): frequency also depends on head area. An app given the wrong head size turns the same sound into a different tension. Compare changes, not absolute values.

📊 4. What the RCS Index Compares, and What It Cannot See

The firmness index of our RCS calculator is computed, not measured, from three inputs: frame stiffness (RA), string stiffness and tension. It does compare two strings or two tensions on one frame. Between two frames it only sees the RA gap: not head size, not pattern, not gauge, not the machine, not the delay.

So two racquets with the same RA get the same index, string and tension being equal:

On a single frame, by contrast, the index separates setups clearly: on an RA 64 frame at 53 lb (24 kg), 24 (comfortable tier) with the Head RIP Control, 32 (firm) with the Head Lynx Tour, 38 (very firm) with the Luxilon 4G (286.9 lb/in, aligned with TWU). Each kilo of tension (about 2 lb) moves it by half a point.

💡 Expert point — changing frames already changes firmness. In the formula, seven RA points weigh as much as 4.8 kg (about 10.6 lb) of tension: with the Head Lynx Tour at 53 lb (24 kg), the index goes from 31 on an RA 62 frame to 34 on an RA 69 frame. Keeping your tension while changing racquets moves firmness before head size even comes into it. This equivalence is internal to the formula, not a measurement.

🎾 5. Can You Really Feel a Tension Difference?

Less well than you would think, in the published protocols. Of 41 advanced recreational players, 27% correctly identified a difference of 11 lb (5 kg) or less, and 37% could not pick a 22 lb (10 kg) difference (Bower and Cross, 2003). Feel alone is an unreliable instrument for comparing two setups.

With earplugs in, only 6 players out of 26 passed the test: part of what we call "feeling the tension" travels through the sound of impact. Among 18 elite players (2008), 61% could not detect a 75 N (17 lb) difference, and 11% detected 25 N (6 lb).

The ball changes little: against a ball machine, 108.1 km/h (67.2 mph) at 180 N (about 40 lb) against 105.3 km/h (65.4 mph) at 280 N (about 63 lb), with more long balls at low tension and more net errors at high tension (2005). One shared limit: these players tested racquets prepared for the study, not their own. These results do not say you feel nothing; they say you are often wrong.

✅ 6. The Protocol: Comparing Two Setups Without Fooling Yourself

Compare measurements, not settings: same instrument, same settings, same delay after stringing. On one frame the comparison is direct. Between two frames, give each racquet its own reference, then adjust by trial.

  1. Keep three numbers apart: the machine setting, the reading after stringing, the reading after play.
  2. Lock everything else: stringer, machine, string, gauge, stringing method, mains-to-crosses setting.
  3. Measure at the same delay. In the USRSA tests, the stringbed loses 6 to 9% of its stiffness in 24 hours without play.
  4. On one frame, compare directly; the RCS index gives the direction and the order of magnitude before you string.
  5. Between two frames, do not carry the setting over: make a first try, measure it, judge it on court, then change one parameter at a time. The reading of the setup you keep becomes that frame's reference.

As for the habit of stringing the crosses 4 lb (1.8 kg) above the mains on a two-piece job, we found no primary source for it: the guide takes stock. For what happens next, see how long tennis strings last.

❓ FAQ — Comparing Two String Setups

Should I string tighter on a larger head?

The direction is known: at equal tension, longer strings make a softer stringbed, so a larger head tends to call for a higher reference tension. No published measurement we could open says by how much: work by trial, measuring each setup the same way.

How do I measure the tension of my strings?

You cannot read the reference tension back; you measure the stringbed: its vibration frequency (an app or an ERT-type device), how far it gives under a load, or one string's tension with a two-prong gauge. These readings track one racquet over time; they do not compare two different frames.

Does a frequency app give the real tension?

No. Turning a frequency into a tension takes the head area and the mass of the strings, so the value shown depends on what you enter. On one racquet the change is meaningful: 10% less tension means 5% less frequency.

Does a pro's string tension apply to my racquet?

No. A tension figure only means something with the frame, string, machine and delay that go with it, and professionals' frames almost always differ from the retail models. We publish no player tension that is not confirmed by two independent sources.

🏁 Conclusion: One Recommendation per Player

A requested tension does not travel from one setup to another. A measurement repeated under the same conditions does.

  • You are changing racquets: do not carry your number over. Keep the string and the stringer, then adjust the new frame's reference tension by trial, measuring each job at the same delay.
  • You play with two racquets of the same model: have them strung together and check that they give the same reading. According to TWU, two racquets of the same model with the same stringbed stiffness should play the same.
  • You are choosing between two strings: keep the frame and the setting, compare the index in the RCS calculator, then measure.
  • You are comparing with a hitting partner: their setting says nothing about your stringbed. Compare readings taken with the same instrument, or do not compare.

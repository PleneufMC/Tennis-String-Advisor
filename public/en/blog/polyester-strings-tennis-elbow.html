Polyester Strings and Tennis Elbow: Which to Choose if You Keep Poly

The 102 polys in our catalogue sorted by what matters to the elbow — stiffness and the setup's firmness index — not by their score.

📅 October 9, 2026 · updated October 10, 2026 • ⏱️ 8 min read • 🩺 Health

⚠️ Strings are not a treatment

Changing strings changes your equipment; it does not heal a tendon. If the pain lasts, wakes you at night or does not ease with rest, see a doctor or a physiotherapist before changing anything else. This article describes equipment; it gives no medical advice — and no tension "that heals".

🩺 The short version

  • The criterion is stiffness. Our 102 polys range from 164.6 to 265 lb/in (median 217.3). In the same frame, that gap is worth 11 RCS points.
  • The softest: Toroline O-Toro Snap (164.6), Isospeed Cream (165) and Toroline O-Toro (165.7) give an index of 24 in our example; the stiffest, Luxilon 4G and 4G Rough, Babolat Pro Hurricane and Solinco Tour Bite Diamond Rough, give 35.
  • "Comfortable" is not "soft". Yonex Poly Tour Pro has the 2nd best comfort rating of the 18 polys assessed by testers, but it is only the 10th softest (220 lb/in).
  • Tension does not make up for the string: 4 kg (about 9 lb) less removes 2 points; changing string can remove 11.

The usual advice for a sore elbow is to leave polyester. Many players do not: they value the control and spin a monofilament gives them. This article is for them. It does not say which poly is best — our 2026 ranking does that — but which one loads your setup the least.

🔎 1. Why sort by stiffness, not by score

A string's stiffness, in lb/in, says how much it resists being stretched: the higher it is, the harder the string. It is the only string property used by the site's RCS index, which combines frame stiffness (RA), string stiffness and tension into one firmness index of the setup, from "very comfortable" (below 20) to "very firm" (35 and above, the risk zone for the elbow).

A comfort rating, on the other hand, is an assessment: a tester describes a feeling at impact, which also depends on the gauge, tension and frame they used. It is useful, but it does not measure the same thing. For a sensitive arm, we therefore sort by stiffness first, and give the RCS index it produces.

In the tables below, the index is computed for one shared example: string at 22 kg (48.5 lb), in three racquets with an RA of 60, 65 and 70. It is not a recommended tension; it is a fixed point to compare strings with each other.

A note on method. A product page often groups several gauges under a single stiffness. When that stiffness is aligned on a measurement by the Tennis Warehouse University (TWU) lab, we show the one for the stiffest gauge TWU measured among the page's gauges. That can overstate the stiffness of a finer gauge, so the RCS index may look severe for that gauge. This is deliberate, out of caution for the arm. For your exact setup, the configurator's index is the one to trust.

At the other end, 19 polys in the catalogue are at 240 lb/in or more. In an RA 65 racquet at 22 kg, they give an index of 32 to 35; in an RA 70 frame, 34 to 37, and most of them then reach the "very firm" threshold (35 and above).

These are not bad strings: several rank very well with testers, for their control and durability. But if your elbow complains, this is the list where changing string pays off most.

⚖️ 4. Felt comfort versus stiffness: the gap

The clearest case is Yonex Poly Tour Pro: 2nd for comfort, but 10th for softness, stiffer than Head Lynx Tour (210 lb/in). Testers describe it as round and soft at impact; its stiffness sits just above the catalogue median (217.3 lb/in). Conversely, Signum Pro X-Perience is one of the softest of the 18 but only 10th (tied) for comfort. The synthesis says so itself: comfort there is often inferred from how a string is described — soft or firm, round or shaped — rather than observed directly.

Both pieces of information are true at once: one describes what the player feels, the other what the string does. For a sensitive arm, we look at stiffness first, because it is what the RCS index uses and because it describes the string, not a feeling. The two polys that stand out in both tables are Solinco Mach-10 and Toroline O-Toro.

🔧 5. String, frame, tension: the order of the levers

The RCS formula lets us compare the three levers on a worked example. In an RA 65 racquet, with Luxilon 4G at 22 kg (index 35):

  • Change the string to Toroline O-Toro, same racquet, same tension: 35 → 24, 11 points.
  • Change the frame to an RA 60, same string, same tension: 35 → 33. Conversely, an RA 70 frame takes the 4G up to 37.
  • Lower the tension by 4 kg (about 9 lb), same racquet, same string: 35 → 33.

In this formula, 1 kg of tension is worth half an index point, one RA point a third of a point, and 10 lb/in of string stiffness a little over one point. The string is the main lever, by far. We give no tension "for the elbow": no measured source sets one, and tension moves the index only a little.

🧵 6. What about hybrids?

Stringing a polyester in part of the racquet and a multifilament in the other is an option often mentioned for keeping some poly. We do not put numbers on it here: only 6 of our 45 multifilaments have a stiffness aligned on a lab measurement, and a pre-packed set has no measurement of its own in our database; an index computed on them would show a precision it does not have. For string families softer than polyester, see our guide to the best tennis strings for tennis elbow and our polyester vs multifilament comparison.

⚠️ History of tennis elbow

Keeping a polyester is a playing choice, not a health choice. Even the softest in this table is still a monofilament: generally firmer than a multifilament, and clearly firmer than natural gut. If your elbow has already been affected, the safest route is still to leave polyester; this article only serves to limit the damage if you do not.

❓ FAQ — polyester strings and tennis elbow

Which polyester string should I use with tennis elbow?

If you keep a polyester, pick one of the softest. In our catalogue of 102 polys, the three least stiff are Toroline O-Toro Snap (164.6 lb/in), Isospeed Cream (165) and Toroline O-Toro (165.7). In a racquet with an RA of 65 strung at 22 kg, they give an RCS index of 24, against 35 for the stiffest. In general, a multifilament or natural gut remains a more prudent choice than a polyester.

Is a polyester rated as comfortable necessarily soft?

No. Yonex Poly Tour Pro has the 2nd best comfort rating of the 18 polys covered by tester reviews, but it is only the 10th softest (220 lb/in). Feel at impact and stiffness do not say the same thing; the RCS index only uses stiffness.

Is lowering the tension enough to protect the elbow with a polyester?

Not according to the site's RCS index: 4 kg (about 9 lb) less removes 2 points, while replacing Luxilon 4G (265 lb/in) with Toroline O-Toro (165.7 lb/in) removes 11 in the same frame. The string choice matters far more than the tension. Strings are not a treatment: persistent pain is a matter for a doctor.
